# Reptiles, Hybrid Evolution, and Conservation Realities at COP17 Armenia

By Mikael Yamiosei

Sitting quietly in the middle rows of the conference hall, I listened as the presenter—a leading Armenian herpetologist—stepped up to the podium for her session. Unlike the bustling exhibition booths outside in the green zone, this room was dedicated to an overlooked facet of the Caucasus biodiversity hotspot: the reptiles of Armenia. While global delegations at COP17 debated high-altitude climate policy, this presentation delivered a stark, ground-level reminder of what is quietly vanishing across our landscapes.




Out of 103 reptile species recognized across the wider Caucasus region, Armenia alone harbors 51 species—spanning tortoises, lizards, and snakes (with crocodiles represented solely within the confines of Yerevan’s zoological park). Yet, as the speaker emphasized from the outset, these creatures remain starkly underrepresented in public education and environmental conservation frameworks.




### Defining the Reptilian Shield: Physiology and Diversity

The presentation began with a fundamental biological clarification often misunderstood by the public. Reptiles are strictly defined by their dry, scale- or scute-covered skin, which allows them to remain independent of damp or aquatic environments. Unlike amphibians, reptiles are never slimy or moist to the touch; their integumentary shielding is an evolutionary adaptation designed strictly for moisture retention.




Within this broad classification, Armenia’s herpetofauna divides into three major orders, each facing distinct ecological pressures.




### Chelonians: The Precarious Status of Armenia’s Turtles

Armenia records three species of turtles and tortoises, whose biology and conservation challenges reveal deep human-wildlife conflicts:




- **Terrestrial Tortoises (Mediterranean Tortoise / _Testudo graeca_):** Herbivorous by nature, these tortoises feed on leaves, grasses, and wild fruit. The presenter detailed how sexual dimorphism can be identified via the male’s concave plastron (lower shell), which facilitates mounting. Distributed primarily across northern regions, the Ararat Plain, and the Meghri area, terrestrial tortoises face severe pressures. Many are captured as domestic pets, despite the fact that captive care is notoriously difficult: tortoises require intense natural sunlight and improper indoor conditions quickly prove fatal. Furthermore, the presenter highlighted a critical ecological hazard: southern tortoise populations are genetically distinct from northern ones and possess different hibernation instincts (southern tortoises burrow deep into the earth, while northern ones shelter under tree bark). Releasing a pet tortoise into the wrong region mixes these distinct gene pools, causing relocated southern tortoises to fail at deep burrowing and freeze during harsh winters. Consequently, unwanted pets should never be released into the wild, but rather surrendered to a zoo.
- **Water Turtles:** Unlike their terrestrial counterparts, aquatic turtles in Armenia are strictly carnivorous, feeding on fish and aquatic fauna rather than vegetation.

### Lizards: From Ancient Relicts to Evolutionary Marvels

Armenia’s lizard diversity spans rugged rock-dwellers, subterranean specialists, and evolutionary anomalies that have captured international scientific attention:




- **Caucasian Rock Agama (_Paralaudakia caucasia_):** Inhabiting high rock faces, these large lizards are frequently misidentified by locals as prehistoric "mini-dinosaurs" or dragons due to their striking size and exposed roadside habitats.
- **Toad-Headed Lizards / Round-Headed Lizards (_Phrynocephalus_):** Highly specialized inhabitants of sandy ecosystems (such as the Ararat region), these populations are facing severe collapse. Human commercial exploitation—specifically sand mining for construction—is actively destroying their native sand-dune habitats. Similarly, Chernov’s toad-headed lizard and other restricted-range taxa survive in only a handful of fragmented localities.
- **Multicolored Lizard (_Eremias arguta_):** This low-altitude species migrated upward along valley floors to the shores of Lake Sevan. Today, a critical population survives adjacent to an active municipal garbage dump, where rats and predators prey upon them. If the dump is closed or modified without ecological oversight, this unique population could vanish entirely.
- **Little Asia Lizard (_Parvilacerta_):** Classified as critically endangered, its remaining populations are heavily fragmented by agricultural land conversion.
- **Green Lizards:** The presentation distinguished between multiple morphologically similar green lizard species in Armenia, including stripe-necked variants, the agile lizard, and the middle lizard (_Lacerta media_), which prefers higher alpine meadows.
- **Parthenogenetic Rock Lizards (Unisexual Evolution):** Armenia holds a legendary status in evolutionary biology because it was here that **parthenogenesis** in vertebrates was first discovered. In these all-female populations, males do not exist; reproduction occurs without fertilization. The presenter explained how this discovery revolutionized global evolutionary theory, proving that new species can emerge rapidly through hybridization. In Armenia’s landscape—particularly around glacial refugia such as Mount Ararat during ice ages—migrating populations were forced together, resulting in genome mixing that generated unisexual species, triploids, and even tetraploids in a remarkably short evolutionary window. Today, Armenia hosts 5 bisexual and 5 parthenogenetic rock lizard species, presenting complex genetic questions where triploid and even tetraploid specimens continue to blur traditional biological boundaries.
- **Caspian Gecko (_Mediodactylus heterocercus_):** An immigrant species first documented in Armenia during the 1990s (noted as discovered by local researchers like Samvel Pipoyan), these geckos arrived via railway transport and have successfully colonized urban environments, spreading into towns like Ijevan.
- **Snake-Eyed Lizard (_Ophisops elegans_):** Distinguished by its unique fused, transparent eyelids (resembling a snake's inability to blink), this species relies more heavily on olfaction than vision, offering high potential for sensory research.
- **European Glass Lizard / Sheltopusik (_Pseudopus apodus_):** A legless lizard that frequently triggers panic among locals who mistake it for a giant venomous snake. The presenter stressed that it is entirely harmless, possesses no venom, and cannot bite defensively in a dangerous manner.

### Snakes: Myth, Ecology, and Venomous Realities

The presentation dedicated significant time to demystifying Armenia’s snake fauna, separating harmless rodent-controllers from true vipers.




#### Non-Venomous and Harmless Species:

- **Blind Snake (_Typhlops vermicularis_):** A worm-like, subterranean burrower possessing nearly identical head and tail morphology—an evolutionary defense strategy designed to confuse predatory attackers regarding its directional orientation.
- **Caucasian Cat Snake (_Telescopus fallax_):** A beautifully patterned, harmless nocturnal hunter.
- **Diademed Whip Snake / Yellow-Bellied Racer (_Dolichophis caspius_):** Large, agile, and beneficial rodent-eaters that often strike aggressive defensive postures when cornered but pose zero venom threat.
- **Dahl’s Whip Snake / Arrow Snake (_Platyceps najadum_):** An arboreal, tree-climbing species with slender proportions.
- **Leopard Snake (_Zamenis situla_):** Praised by the presenter as arguably the most visually striking and aesthetically stunning non-venomous snake in Armenia.
- **Coin-Marked Snake / _Hemorrhois ravergieri_:** Noted as the most aggressive non-venomous snake in the country; its survival strategy relies entirely on bluffing and intimidation rather than flight. Tragically, it is frequently killed because humans mistake its patterns for venomous vipers.
- **Dice Snake (_Natrix tessellata_) and Grass Snake (_Natrix natrix_):** Aquatic and semi-aquatic specialists easily identified by distinct ear-like neck markings, feeding primarily on fish and frogs.

#### Venomous Species (Vipers):

All venomous snakes in Armenia belong to the family **Viperidae**:




- **Meadow Viper / Steppe Viper (_Vipera ursinii_):** An inhabitant of high-altitude mountain-steppe zones. While its venom is potent, its smaller physical mass delivers lower overall venom yields compared to larger vipers.
- **Levant Viper / Blunt-Nosed Viper (Gurza / _Macrovipera lebetina_):** The most widespread and medically significant venomous snake in Armenia. Because the gurza prefers rocky scrublands overlapping with human agricultural zones, human-snake encounters are most frequent here.

#### Myth-Busting Identification and First Aid:

The presenter provided concrete morphological rules to distinguish venomous vipers from harmless snakes:




1. **Head Scales:** Venomous vipers possess small, fragmented scales across the head, whereas non-venomous snakes feature large, distinct dorsal plates/shields.
2. **Pupils:** Vipers feature vertical, cat-like pupils.
3. **Body Profile:** Vipers typically exhibit a stout, three-dimensional triangular body cross-section, whereas non-venomous racers maintain more cylindrical profiles.

Regarding snakebite first aid, the presenter offered pragmatic, life-saving guidance:




- **Prevention:** Wear proper sturdy boots and trousers when traversing reptile habitats; avoid walking barefoot in gurza territory.
- **Immediate Extraction:** If bitten by a venomous snake, venom should be actively sucked out using the mouth within the first two minutes, which can reduce injected venom load by half. The presenter noted this is safe provided the rescuer has no open oral lacerations.
- **Managing Swelling:** Remove rings, watches, or restrictive clothing immediately before localized tissue swelling sets in.
- **Psychological Control:** Panic must be avoided because elevated heart rates accelerate blood circulation and systemic venom distribution.
- **Medical Realities:** In healthy adults, 90% of untreated snakebites are not fatal. The presenter cautioned against the indiscriminate administration of hospital antivenom serums, noting that mismatched antivenoms or delayed administration (beyond two hours) can compound immune shock and introduce severe secondary medical risks.

### Tying Back to COP17 Armenia: The Evolutionary Blueprint

As I sat in the audience taking notes, the broader connection between this herpetological presentation and COP17 Armenia became sharply evident. International biodiversity negotiations frequently default to macro-level metrics—hectares of forest preserved, carbon sequestration targets, and large-scale protected area expansion. Yet, Armenia’s true global ecological significance lies in its role as a concentrated evolutionary laboratory.




The Caucasus hotspot, and Armenia specifically, contains rare evolutionary phenomena—such as the hybrid speciation and parthenogenetic lineages of rock lizards—that cannot be replicated anywhere else on Earth. These species are frontline biological indicators of climate change. As shifting temperatures force low-altitude species upward and agricultural expansion fragments delicate habitats like sand dunes and alpine meadows, Armenia's reptiles are experiencing acute localized extinctions.




Protecting these unique lineages requires more than political rhetoric at a global summit. It demands rigorous baseline science, habitat preservation that accounts for micro-corridors, and public education that replaces fear with ecological understanding. For COP17 Armenia to achieve a lasting legacy, policymakers must recognize that saving global biodiversity means defending the hidden, fragile evolutionary pathways playing out right beneath our feet in the stones and scrublands of the Caucasus.
