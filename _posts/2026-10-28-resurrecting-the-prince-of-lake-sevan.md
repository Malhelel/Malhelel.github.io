# **How Armenia’s Endemic Trout Restoration Intersects with Global Biodiversity Targets at CBD COP17**

## **By Mikael Yamiosei**

## **The High-Altitude Crossroads: CBD COP17 Meets Lake Sevan’s Ecological Crucible**

As delegates, scientists, and environmental ministers gather in Yerevan for the 17th Conference of the Parties to the UN Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD COP17), the overarching discourse centers on translating high-level global policy—specifically the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework—into measurable, living conservation outcomes. Just an hour north of the diplomatic venues in the capital, Lake Sevan rests nearly 1,900 meters above sea level as the ultimate litmus test for Armenia's ecological commitment. As the largest body of water in the Caucasus and the primary freshwater reservoir of the republic, Sevan is not merely a geographic landmark; it is an isolated alpine ecosystem whose biological health reflects the state of the region's broader aquatic biodiversity.

At the absolute core of this ecosystem's natural identity is the Sevan trout (_Salmo ischchan_), historically revered in Armenian heritage as the _Ishkhan_, or "prince fish." Within the framework of COP17, the ongoing multi-decadal effort to restore _Salmo ischchan_ serves as Armenia's primary case study in freshwater conservation. Rather than treating global biodiversity agreements as passive administrative benchmarks, the Sevan restoration illustrates the tangible mechanics of species preservation: how targeted ecological intervention, genetic management, and watershed governance intersect to keep an ancient evolutionary lineage from disappearing entirely from the planet.

## **Evolutionary Architecture: The Four Races of _Salmo ischchan_**

To understand the biological significance of the Sevan trout is to understand ecological speciation within an isolated lacustrine environment. _Salmo ischchan_ did not exist as a single homogenous population; rather, it evolved into four distinct morphological and behavioral races, each precisely adapted to specific thermal gradients, depth profiles, and spawning grounds within Lake Sevan’s 1,270-square-kilometer basin:

- **Gegharkuni (_S. i. gegarkuni_):** A lacustrine-riverine form that feeds predominantly in the open water on zooplankton and macroinvertebrates, migrating into entering rivers to deposit eggs in gravel currents.
- **Summer Trout (_S. i. aestivalis_):** Spawns during warmer months both in shallow littoral zones of the lake and in the lower reaches of feeder streams.
- **Winter Trout (_S. i. ischchan_):** The largest, deep-water race, historically spawning on underwater gravel beds at depths of up to 15 to 20 meters during winter months.
- **Boon (_S. i. danilewskii_):** A dwarf, slow-growing form restricted to deeper, cold lacustrine zones, feeding primarily on benthic organisms.

This evolutionary divergence allowed _Salmo ischchan_ to occupy virtually every major ecological niche in the lake's water column, maintaining top-predator stability and driving trophic energy flow. Preserving this species is not merely a matter of maintaining fish biomass for resource extraction; it is a fundamental requirement for safeguarding the evolutionary diversity that stabilizes Lake Sevan’s food web.

## **The Hydrological Fracture: Mid-Century Disturbance and Subspecies Loss**

The ecological balance of Lake Sevan was violently disrupted during the mid-20th century by massive Soviet-era water diversion projects. Intensive extractions through the Hrazdan River cascade for agricultural irrigation and hydroelectric power generation caused the lake’s water level to plummet by roughly 20 meters over several decades. This drastic reduction in volume and surface area triggered cascading limnological shifts across the entire basin.

The drawdown exposed and dried out the shallow, oxygenated pebble beds in littoral zones where the Winter trout and Boon subspecies reproduced. Deprived of their specialized spawning grounds and subjected to rapid temperature changes in shrinking deep-water refuges, both the wild Winter trout and Boon subspecies suffered complete reproductive failure and are now considered extinct in the wild. Simultaneously, natural reproduction of the remaining Gegharkuni and Summer trout was reduced to negligible levels as entering river mouths dried up or became inaccessible, breaking a multi-thousand-year chain of natural recruitment.

## **Watershed Hurdles: Channel Fragmentation and Trophic Pressures**

Modern restoration efforts must confront severe physical and ecological barriers across Sevan’s watershed. Out of the 28 permanent and seasonal rivers feeding the basin, only a minor fraction currently possess the hydrological flow, gravel substratum, and water purity necessary to support wild salmonid spawning migrations. Many feeder channels remain fragmented by agricultural diversions, municipal runoff, and structural obstructions that block fish from reaching historical upstream spawning gravels.

Furthermore, the introduction of non-native species during the 20th century dramatically altered the lake’s trophic dynamics. The European whitefish (_Coregonus lavaretus_), introduced to compensate for declining commercial fish yields, multiplied rapidly to occupy the open-water pelagic zone. Whitefish compete directly with juvenile Sevan trout for benthic macroinvertebrates and zooplankton, creating intense interspecific pressure. Overcoming these watershed-wide pressures requires active intervention; relying solely on natural recovery under current limnological conditions is an ecological impossibility.

## **Scientific Guardrails: Genetic Integrity in Restocking**

Because wild recruitment remains severely bottlenecked, artificial propagation and stocking are vital to maintaining population levels. However, hydrobiologists and geneticists emphasize that uncontrolled restocking poses severe risks if hatchery practices fail to maintain genetic purity. Indiscriminate breeding or introducing non-native salmonid strains risks diluting the unique genetic architecture that allows _Salmo ischchan_ to thrive in Sevan’s distinct mineral-rich, high-altitude water chemistry.

To prevent genetic erosion, current stocking protocols utilize strict broodstock selection. Biologists extract wild and captive-reared spawners that match precise morphological and genetic markers of pure Gegharkuni and Summer trout lines. By maintaining distinct age cohorts and avoiding inbreeding depression within hatchery facilities, scientists ensure that released fingerlings retain their native evolutionary tolerances—including specialized thermal endurance and specific foraging behaviors—before entering the lake environment.

## **Technical Infrastructure: Onshore RAS and In-Lake Modular Cages**

To operationalize restocking at scale, the Restoration Foundation—established under Government Decision No. 1442-N—utilizes a dual-track aquaculture framework designed to maximize survival rates while safeguarding water quality:

[Onshore Broodstock & RAS Hatchery]

  └── Karchaghbyur Fingerling Plant (\~1.5M fry/cycle; 90% water recycling)

        │

        ▼

[In-Lake Acclimatization Cages]

  └── Sevan Aqua CJSC Floating Net-Pens (Natural current & thermal adjustment)

        │

        ▼

[Systematic Lake & River Stocking]

- **Onshore Biotechnology (Karchaghbyur Fingerling Plant):** Operating as the technological core of the program, this facility employs Recirculating Aquaculture Systems (RAS). Advanced biofilters remove nitrogenous waste, inject dissolved oxygen, and recycle up to 90 percent of process water daily. This closed-loop environment yields approximately 1.5 million genetically verified fry per cycle while preventing nutrient-rich hatchery effluent from polluting local waterways.
- **In-Lake Acclimatization (Sevan Aqua CJSC Cages):** Before full release into the open lake, juvenile trout are transferred to modular floating net-pen cages anchored directly in Lake Sevan. This intermediate stage allows young fish to adjust naturally to ambient water temperatures, seasonal thermal stratification, micro-currents, and live prey dynamics, significantly reducing mortality rates upon wild release.

## **Governance on the Water: Anti-Poaching and Resource Protection**

Propagating millions of fingerlings achieves little if adult fish are harvested before reaching reproductive maturity. Unregulated commercial extraction and rampant poaching historically decimated returning adult broodstock at river mouths during spawning runs. In response, state authorities established a coordinated enforcement network combining the specialized Water Police unit and expanded Eco-Patrol Services.

During critical autumn and winter spawning windows, these agencies enforce strict seasonal fishing bans across the entire lake surface and deploy continuous patrols along key river mouths, such as the Karchaghbyur, Masrik, and Argitchi rivers. Confiscating illegal monofilament netting and blocking unauthorized boat launches protects maturing Gegharkuni and Summer trout, allowing stock biomass to rebuild and safeguarding released cohorts until they reach adult breeding age.

## **Community Integration: Riparian Stewardship and Local Economies**

Long-term biodiversity conservation is unsustainable if enforced strictly through top-down policing against local populations. The Ministry of Environment, alongside international development partners, has increasingly focused on aligning the socio-economic interests of riparian communities in Gegharkunik Province with the ecological health of the lake.

Programs directed at villages surrounding Lake Sevan seek to transition informal harvesters away from illegal fishing toward alternative, sustainable livelihoods. Financial support and technical training are funneled into eco-tourism ventures, sustainable agriculture, and community-based environmental monitoring. By hiring local residents to manage riparian buffer zones and participate in river restoration monitoring, conservation transforms from an external regulatory imposition into a locally owned economic asset.

## **Global Biodiversity Resonance: Local Action in the CBD COP17 Framework**

The recovery of the Sevan trout demonstrates how localized, high-altitude conservation directly aligns with global biodiversity mandates. Within the CBD COP17 framework, international partners have recognized Lake Sevan as a high-priority freshwater ecosystem requiring sustained multilateral support.

This work directly advances key pillars of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (KMGBF), particularly **Target 2** (ensuring at least 30 percent of degraded inland water ecosystems are under effective restoration) and **Target 4** (halting human-induced extinction of known threatened species and maintaining genetic diversity). Major international mechanisms—such as the European Union's _EU4BiodiversityinSevan_ project (€1.5 million in dedicated funding spanning through 2029)—provide direct technical assistance, water-quality monitoring equipment, and management support to Sevan National Park and the trout restoration program. By integrating rigorous conservation science, state-level enforcement, modern aquaculture technology, and community participation, Armenia’s work on _Salmo ischchan_ provides a concrete, real-world model of how nations can arrest species decline and restore damaged aquatic ecosystems.
