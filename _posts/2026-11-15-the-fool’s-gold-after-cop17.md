### Armenia’s Amulsar Mine and the Unresolved Questions of the Yerevan Summit

<strong>By Mikael Yamiosei</strong>

_Standfirst: Published after COP17 concluded in Yerevan on 31 October 2026, this article examines how the Amulsar gold mine — opened nine days before the summit — became a test of Armenia’s environmental credibility and a contradiction at the heart of the “Yerevan Pathway.”_

***

### 1. A Calendar That Argued With Itself

On the tenth of October, 2026, Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan travelled to the hills above Jermuk to cut the ribbon on the Amulsar gold mine. He told those gathered that the project was a national achievement — a source of skilled work, tax revenue and foreign capital, and proof that Armenia could deliver large industrial undertakings after years of delay.[^1] Nine days later, on the nineteenth of October, the seventeenth Conference of the Parties to the United Nations Convention on Biological Diversity opened in Yerevan, with Armenia in the chair.[^2] No editorialising is required to notice the sequence. The country spent one week celebrating an open-pit gold operation in a mountain watershed and the next week convening the world to discuss how to stop the loss of exactly that kind of landscape. COP17 ran one day beyond its scheduled close, concluding at mid-morning on the thirty-first of October with the adoption of the “Yerevan Pathway.”[^3] The contradiction had not been resolved by the summit; it had acquired a diplomatic name.

The response from Armenian civil society was swift and unusually broad. More than four dozen non-governmental organisations signed a joint statement condemning the timing and substance of the launch, arguing that a state hosting a biodiversity summit cannot simultaneously permit a new industrial extractive project in a zone of recognised ecological value without fatally undermining its own agenda.[^4] Their objections were not confined to symbolism. The statement held that proceeding with the mine conflicts with Armenia’s undertakings under the Convention on Biological Diversity and the Council of Europe’s Bern Convention, and that it makes the country’s stated commitment to the Kunming-Montreal framework difficult to reconcile with its domestic permitting decisions.

That argument acquires force from the legal record, which is not a matter of interpretation, though it is more contradictory than partisans on either side tend to acknowledge. On 2 December 2024, an Administrative Court panel presided over by Judge Karen Zarikyan partially upheld a nine-year-old lawsuit brought by residents of Gndevaz and two NGOs, finding that the environmental impact assessment had been obtained “in violation of the law” and that the process itself was “merely formalistic.”[^5] The court declared null and void specific ministerial orders and amendments relating to the expansion of the subsoil site and the modification of the extraction permit’s term.[^6] Yet the operative permits themselves were not annulled. In a decision dated 25 December 2024, the Administrative Court held that the subsoil-use permits granted to Lydian Armenia, including their amendments, constitute **unappealable administrative acts** and cannot be revoked either by the administration or by the courts.[^7] The government has cited this finding in its submission to the Bern Convention, stating that it currently has no mechanism to suspend or invalidate the permits. The outcome is not a single paradox but two courts answering two different questions: one found the assessment _process_ unlawful, the other found the _permits_ immune from challenge. The mine opened on the space between those answers.

The government’s answer to these concerns has rested on two claims: that the relevant studies demonstrate minimal environmental impact, and that the public now has a direct financial stake in the venture. In late 2024 the state acquired 12.5 per cent of the project, a share officials presented as a mechanism for channelling mining revenue toward public needs.[^8] Yet the state also underwrote up to $150 million in loans to finish construction — a guarantee that environmental and anti-corruption organisations challenged in court.[^9] A regulator that stands to collect dividends and carries contingent liability for the venture’s debts is not positioned to judge it dispassionately. This is a structural conflict of interest, not merely a rhetorical point.

The widest frame belongs to the Global Forest Coalition, which reads Amulsar as an illustration of a problem larger than one mine. The project’s environmental planning included a biodiversity offset programme intended to compensate for significant residual impacts of the mine.[^10] Offsets work by allowing a developer to destroy habitat in one place on the condition that equivalent habitat is protected or restored elsewhere. The Coalition’s objection is that this treats an irreplaceable watershed as a ledger entry that can be balanced, and that in practice the offset scheme financed by the project’s lenders served to legitimise a project that should not have proceeded.[^11] Whether or not one accepts that framing in full, the observation is difficult to dismiss: Armenia is not an anomaly but an example, and the distance between what a state says at a summit and what it signs at a mine site is precisely what the summit exists to narrow.
