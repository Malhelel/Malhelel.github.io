\## \<strong>Armenia’s Amulsar Mine and the Unresolved Questions of the Yerevan Summit\</strong>

\<strong>By Mikael Yamiosei\</strong>

\*Standfirst: Published after COP17 concluded in Yerevan on 31 October 2026, this article examines how the Amulsar gold mine — opened nine days before the summit — became a test of Armenia’s environmental credibility and a contradiction at the heart of the “Yerevan Pathway.”\*

\---

\### \<strong>1. A Calendar That Argued With Itself\</strong>

On the tenth of October, 2026, Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan travelled to the hills above Jermuk to cut the ribbon on the Amulsar gold mine. He told those gathered that the project was a national achievement — a source of skilled work, tax revenue and foreign capital, and proof that Armenia could deliver large industrial undertakings after years of delay. Nine days later, on the nineteenth of October, the seventeenth Conference of the Parties to the United Nations Convention on Biological Diversity opened in Yerevan, with Armenia in the chair. No editorialising is required to notice the sequence. The country spent one week celebrating an open-pit gold operation in a mountain watershed and the next week convening the world to discuss how to stop the loss of exactly that kind of landscape. COP17 ran one day beyond its scheduled close, concluding at mid-morning on the thirty-first of October with the adoption of the “Yerevan Pathway.” The contradiction had not been resolved by the summit; it had acquired a diplomatic name.

The response from Armenian civil society was swift and unusually broad. More than four dozen non-governmental organisations signed a joint statement condemning the timing and substance of the launch, arguing that a state hosting a biodiversity summit cannot simultaneously permit a new industrial extractive project in a zone of recognised ecological value without fatally undermining its own agenda. Their objections were not confined to symbolism. The statement held that proceeding with the mine conflicts with Armenia’s undertakings under the Convention on Biological Diversity and the Council of Europe’s Bern Convention, and that it makes the country’s stated commitment to the Kunming-Montreal framework difficult to reconcile with its domestic permitting decisions.

That argument acquires force from the legal record, which is not a matter of interpretation, though it is more contradictory than partisans on either side tend to acknowledge. On 2 December 2024, an Administrative Court panel presided over by Judge Karen Zarikyan partially upheld a nine-year-old lawsuit brought by residents of Gndevaz and two NGOs, finding that the environmental impact assessment had been obtained “in violation of the law” and that the process itself was “merely formalistic.” The court declared null and void specific ministerial orders and amendments relating to the expansion of the subsoil site and the modification of the extraction permit’s term. Yet the operative permits themselves were not annulled. In a decision dated 25 December 2024, the Administrative Court held that the subsoil-use permits granted to Lydian Armenia, including their amendments, constitute \*\*unappealable administrative acts\*\* and cannot be revoked either by the administration or by the courts. The government has cited this finding in its submission to the Bern Convention, stating that it currently has no mechanism to suspend or invalidate the permits. The outcome is not a single paradox but two courts answering two different questions: one found the assessment \*process\* unlawful, the other found the \*permits\* immune from challenge. The mine opened on the space between those answers.

The government’s answer to these concerns has rested on two claims: that the relevant studies demonstrate minimal environmental impact, and that the public now has a direct financial stake in the venture. In late 2024 the state acquired 12.5 per cent of the project, a share officials presented as a mechanism for channelling mining revenue toward public needs. Yet the state also underwrote up to $150 million in loans to finish construction — a guarantee that environmental and anti-corruption organisations challenged in court. A regulator that stands to collect dividends and carries contingent liability for the venture’s debts is not positioned to judge it dispassionately. This is a structural conflict of interest, not merely a rhetorical point.

The widest frame belongs to the Global Forest Coalition, which reads Amulsar as an illustration of a problem larger than one mine. The project’s environmental planning included a biodiversity offset programme intended to compensate for significant residual impacts of the mine. Offsets work by allowing a developer to destroy habitat in one place on the condition that equivalent habitat is protected or restored elsewhere. The Coalition’s objection is that this treats an irreplaceable watershed as a ledger entry that can be balanced, and that in practice the offset scheme financed by the project’s lenders served to legitimise a project that should not have proceeded. Whether or not one accepts that framing in full, the observation is difficult to dismiss: Armenia is not an anomaly but an example, and the distance between what a state says at a summit and what it signs at a mine site is precisely what the summit exists to narrow.

\### \<strong>2. What the Ground Itself Suggests\</strong>

To understand the objections, it helps to start with hydrology rather than politics. Amulsar sits in Vayots Dzor province, in high terrain whose streams drain into the Arpa and Vorotan river systems. Those rivers are not peripheral waterways. They feed the Kechut Reservoir and supply the tunnel system that carries water toward Lake Sevan, Armenia’s largest freshwater body. Activists describe the surrounding highlands as the operational centre of the country’s water management, and the description is not rhetorical excess — it is a reasonable summary of the engineering that connects these mountains to the national water supply.

The industrial process raises the first set of questions. Amulsar is designed around cyanide heap leaching, in which ore is crushed, stacked and irrigated with a chemical solution that dissolves gold. The leach facility, according to critics who have examined the project’s own documentation, would sit partly over the catchment of a stream feeding the Arpa. That means a liner failure, a pond overtopping during exceptional rainfall, or a burst pipe during solution transport would have a direct route into flowing water. Cyanide does not wait for permitting hearings; it moves with the current.

A second and slower hazard is acid rock drainage. The project will generate enormous volumes of waste rock. The published estimates do not measure identical quantities: project literature cites approximately 302 million tonnes of waste rock over the life of the mine; an Armenian CSO statement estimates 250–300 million tonnes of overburden and waste rock; and EcoLur’s president has cited approximately 150 million tonnes of waste rock that will accumulate. The figures should not be treated as competing measurements of a single stockpile, but the order of magnitude is beyond dispute. What is not in dispute is that the material is sulfide-bearing: once excavated and exposed to air and moisture, it oxidises to produce sulfuric acid, which mobilises metals held in the stone. The 2019 review conducted by the consultancy ELARD, under contract to Armenia’s Investigative Committee within a criminal case concerning the permitting process, identified errors in the project’s acid drainage calculations and weaknesses in its water modelling. The criminal case itself was terminated in December 2021 for absence of \*corpus delicti\*, but the forensic findings were not withdrawn and stand on their own as expert conclusions produced for a state investigation.

The biodiversity dimension follows from the same geography. The Bern Convention’s Bureau has recommended that Jermuk National Park be established in a form that would encompass Mount Amulsar, citing habitats and species falling under the Emerald Network, and has asked Armenian authorities to suspend permits for subsoil exploitation until a new, comprehensive assessment is complete. A mine does not merely occupy a footprint; it introduces roads, crushing plants, conveyor corridors, waste dumps and settling ponds, and it severs the connections that allow animal populations to move and plants to disperse. The offset logic is sharpest here: a habitat can be recreated on paper, but a contaminated river cannot be offset.

The final ecological problem is one of duration. Gold extraction at Amulsar may run for a decade or so; the consequences of acid drainage and heavy-metal mobilisation can persist for generations. Some opponents of the project speak of pollution lasting five centuries, a figure that should be treated as an estimate advanced by campaigners rather than a settled scientific finding. Even discounted for caution, however, the asymmetry holds. The revenues are counted in annual budget cycles. The liabilities are counted in geological time.

\### \<strong>3. The Towns That Were Not Asked\</strong>

The communities nearest Amulsar did not arrive at their position recently. In 2018, in the wake of the Velvet Revolution, residents of Jermuk and neighbouring settlements closed the roads to the construction site and held them for the better part of two years. The blockade was not a single protest that got out of hand; it was a sustained campaign with logistical organisation, rotating participation and a clear demand. It ended only when the 2020 war with Azerbaijan consumed national attention, and even then the underlying objections did not disappear — they lost their audience.

Those objections are economic before they are environmental. Jermuk’s identity rests on mineral springs, sanatoria and the expectation of clean air and clean water. Residents have argued, in meetings and in letters, that converting a health resort into a mining district trades a durable local economy for a temporary one. A formulation that circulated among them during the 2023 debates over financing captures the reasoning: they were not prepared to surrender two thousand existing jobs for the sake of a thousand new ones. That arithmetic can be disputed, but it deserves to be engaged rather than dismissed.

Health is the second axis of local complaint. Survey work among Jermuk residents during the construction period, reported in 2018, found that 85.7 per cent of respondents described respiratory and other complaints — asthma, chronic lung conditions, headaches, sleeplessness — which they associated with dust from earthworks and haulage. The survey was not a representative sample: it covered 35 households across the three communities closest to the mine, and the figure should be read as an indication of perceived harm rather than a prevalence estimate. It is also not possible to establish from such self-reported data a causal link to the mine, and this article does not assert one. What matters is that residents themselves made that connection at the time, before any cyanide had been applied to ore, and that their perception of harm predates the operational phase entirely.

The procedural grievance is more concrete still. Residents maintain they were not meaningfully consulted during the preparation of the 2015–2016 environmental and social impact assessment, and that no adequate consultation occurred before the 2026 launch. Petitions opposing metal mining in the community’s territory have been signed by residents of Jermuk and surrounding settlements, though the exact number of signatories varies across published accounts and no single verified figure is available. Whatever one thinks of the mine’s technical merits, a project that opens over the documented objections of the nearest residents, without a fresh assessment incorporating their evidence, has a legitimacy problem that no ribbon-cutting can solve.

One further observation belongs here, and it is uncomfortable. For this article, attempts were made to speak with residents directly — through a Google Forms questionnaire, through email, and through local guide contacts on WhatsApp. The response was not hostile; it was absent. Not one person was willing to be named, or to have an opinion attributed to them, even anonymously in substance. This is not a gap in reporting to be papered over. It is a finding, and it suggests something about how costly it currently feels to speak on the record about Amulsar in the communities closest to it.

\### \<strong>4. Why a Government Wants a Gold Mine\</strong>

It is worth stating the case for Amulsar plainly, because the project does not exist for frivolous reasons. Armenia is a small, landlocked economy with limited export variety, a persistent revenue gap and regions where formal employment is scarce. Pashinyan and his ministers present the mine as infrastructure for development: well-paid technical jobs, a stream of taxes and royalties, and the signal to foreign investors that the country can see a major project through. Officials have put the expected annual fiscal contribution at roughly $120–130 million, describing it as worth well over a percentage point of national growth.

The strongest version of the government’s argument is a sovereign development dilemma. The choice, on this account, is not between “gold and water” in the abstract but between a revenue stream that funds public services and a set of environmental risks that modern engineering can manage rather than eliminate. Every industrial activity carries risk; the question is whether the risk is acceptable and whether the state can regulate it effectively. The government’s answer is that Armenia cannot afford to forgo the revenue, that the project meets international standards, and that the state’s equity stake ensures the public captures the upside. This is a coherent position, and it deserves to be tested rather than caricatured. The difficulty is that the regulatory record — a court finding that the EIA process was unlawful, a Bern Convention request for suspension, a forensic review documenting calculation errors — does not support the claim that the risk is being effectively managed.

The ownership arrangement reflects a deliberate political choice. The project is operated by Lydian Armenia, which is now 87.5 per cent held by an international mining group, with the Armenian state holding the remaining 12.5 per cent. That residual share was acquired in 2024 and presented as a way of ensuring the public captures some of the upside. Alongside it sits the loan guarantee of up to $150 million, which was intended to unblock construction financing after earlier arrangements collapsed. Between equity and guarantee, the state has bound its own balance sheet to the mine’s fortunes.

The corporate lineage is worth following, because it explains some of the suspicion. Lydian Armenia is a subsidiary of Lydian International, a company registered in Jersey and formerly listed in Toronto. The company’s own published material notes that Jersey offers a simplified tax environment while insisting that its Armenian entity pays all applicable Armenian taxes and royalties. That may well be accurate. But when a project’s environmental risks sit in one jurisdiction and its corporate structure in another, critics will ask who bears the downside if things go wrong — and the honest answer is that the answer is not obvious from the public record.

At the inauguration, Pashinyan told the audience that “the subsoil belongs to the people of the Republic of Armenia” and that “this wealth will serve the country’s development and prosperity.” The ceremony featured the presentation of a gold bar produced through the full industrial cycle. Critics and analysts have read the timing — nine days before COP17 — and the symbolism of the gold bar as an assertion of sovereign control over natural resources, a message that Armenia sets its own development terms and will not accept environmental conditionality as a veto. That reading is contested by the government, which frames the launch as an economic and legal necessity rather than a geopolitical statement.

Underneath all of this sits a question the government has not answered publicly: what is this land for? For Jermuk, the answer has historically been water — mineral springs, resort economy, agriculture in the valleys below. For the national budget, the answer is now gold. Those are different answers, and choosing between them is a political act rather than a technical one. The state has made its choice. It has not offered a full account of what that choice costs, or of who will be paying.

\### \<strong>5. Counting What the Mine Gives\</strong>

The employment figures deserve careful handling, because they are cited in different forms by different parties. The government has stated that the operation currently employs around 1,600 people and supports several thousand more jobs indirectly through suppliers and local spending. The company’s own earlier commitments referenced at least 700 permanent positions once the mine reached steady-state operation, with construction employment peaking higher. These numbers are not necessarily contradictory — construction and operation are different phases — but they are frequently deployed as if interchangeable, which muddies rather than clarifies the picture.

The fiscal claim is more straightforward in its headline form. Annual payments to the state budget in taxes, duties and royalties are projected in the $120–130 million range, a figure that would place the operating entity among Armenia’s larger corporate taxpayers. For a country that struggles to fund public services from a narrow revenue base, a contribution of that magnitude is not trivial, and it is reasonable for officials to point it out. It is also reasonable to ask what that revenue is buying, and at what price.

The company also points to community investment, describing its social programmes not as charity but as partnerships intended to build lasting local capacity — roads, water infrastructure, gas connections, support for agriculture and education. Such commitments are common in modern mining and should be reported as stated intentions rather than verified outcomes; the difference matters. If they are delivered, they represent genuine value to communities that have often been underserved by state investment. If they lapse when the ore runs out, they will be remembered as a leasing arrangement rather than a partnership.

What is missing is a full accounting on the other side of the ledger. The $120–130 million figure is gross. A complete cost-benefit analysis would need to itemise the public capital committed to the equity stake, the contingent liability created by the $150 million guarantee, the cost of monitoring and enforcement over decades, the eventual expense of closure and remediation — which in most jurisdictions falls to the state when an operator is no longer present — and the value of ecosystem services lost if the watershed is degraded. It would also need to assign a monetary figure to the health and livelihood risks borne by the communities nearest the site. None of these items appears in any published Armenian assessment. Until one does, the benefits are itemised and the costs are not.

Finally, the geography of benefit and burden does not line up. The tax revenue is national; the employment is partly local and partly specialised labour brought in from elsewhere; the environmental exposure is concentrated almost entirely in the Arpa and Vorotan watersheds and in the towns immediately below the site. The residents who oppose the mine are not rejecting development in the abstract. They are asking why their water and their health should underwrite a revenue stream that the country could, in principle, obtain through activities that do not require them to absorb the risk.

\### \<strong>6. A Decade of Stopping and Starting\</strong>

The Amulsar deposit was identified in the mid-2000s, when British and Armenian geologists confirmed a commercially interesting gold and silver resource. The project was listed in Toronto in 2008 to raise capital, and construction began in October 2016, with first production anticipated two years later. On paper, this was an unremarkable trajectory for a mid-sized mine in a post-Soviet economy: foreign capital, international engineering standards, export revenue and a government eager to see it happen.

Then came 2018. The Velvet Revolution changed the political weather, and the roadblocks went up. For more than two years the access roads to Amulsar were closed, construction stopped, and the dispute became a test case for whether a newly mobilised civil society could actually halt an industrial project. The blockade only broke when the 2020 war demanded the country’s full attention. In the years since, opponents of the mine have frequently described that sequence — the protest, the war, the slow resumption — as the moment their campaign lost its momentum rather than its argument.

The financial consequences for the original developer were severe. Lydian International entered creditor protection proceedings in late 2019, and the project passed through restructuring into new ownership. The current structure, with the international group holding 87.5 per cent and the Armenian state holding the remainder, is the product of that unwinding rather than of a deliberate industrial strategy. In 2023 the government signed a memorandum of understanding with the operator and a development bank envisaging a substantial financing package; when that did not materialise, the state loan guarantee took its place.

The legal chronology runs parallel and is equally relevant to how the mine came to open. Beyond the Administrative Court’s 2024 finding that the EIA process was unlawful, the Bern Convention’s Bureau has maintained a consistent position that a new, comprehensive assessment should precede further subsoil permits. The ELARD review, commissioned by the Investigative Committee within a criminal case, documented multiple errors in the project’s environmental documentation; the case itself was terminated in December 2021 without charges, but the forensic findings were not retracted. The mine nevertheless began operations in October 2026. Whatever the merits of the underlying science, the procedural record is one of unresolved objections rather than settled questions.

There is a longer history here, too, and it is the one least represented in the coverage. The Arpa and Vorotan valleys have supported settlement and cultivation for millennia; Jermuk’s springs have been valued for centuries as a curative resource. The gold in Mount Amulsar is older still, but it lay undisturbed through all of that human history, and the decision to extract it is a recent, contingent, political choice. The mine is a temporary intervention in a landscape that will outlast it. That distinction — between what is permanent and what is merely profitable — is the one the historical record keeps insisting on.

\### \<strong>7. How the Gold Comes Out\</strong>

The technical heart of the project is heap leaching, and it is worth explaining without either mystification or alarmism. Ore is blasted, dug and crushed to a coarse gravel — the design specifies fragments passing a nineteen-millimetre screen. That material is stacked in lifts on an engineered pad lined with impermeable membrane, and a dilute sodium cyanide solution is trickled through the pile. The solution percolates down, dissolves gold and silver, and is collected from beneath the pad as a pregnant leach solution, from which the metals are recovered. A leaching cycle runs for roughly two months before the spent ore is left in place and a new lift is stacked.

The operator presents this as a controlled and conventional design. Its published documentation describes a lined pad, a solution collection system, a recovery plant and a fixed conveyor carrying crushed ore from the crusher to the leach area — the conveyor being preferable to truck haulage in dust and traffic terms. Judged purely as engineering, heap leaching is a legitimate method used at hundreds of operations worldwide, and it is chosen precisely because it avoids the fine grinding and large tailings impoundments that tank leaching requires. The company’s position is that the design meets accepted international practice.

The difficulty is that accepted practice is a statement about method, not about site. The risks of heap leaching are concentrated in containment: liner integrity, pond capacity, pipework, and the behaviour of the pad under stress. Where a leach facility overlies the catchment of a flowing stream, as critics of this project maintain, containment ceases to be a technical detail and becomes the central question. And the forensic review commissioned within the criminal case found the project’s water modelling and acid drainage calculations wanting — which means the documents asserting that containment is adequate are themselves of contested reliability.

Site conditions sharpen the concern considerably. The Amulsar area is recognised as among the country’s more significant landslide zones and lies in terrain with meaningful seismic exposure. A leach pad is a heavy, saturated, engineered structure; a slope failure or a strong earthquake is exactly the kind of event that tests liners and pipework beyond design assumptions. The Administrative Court’s criticism of the assessment process as formalistic bears directly on this point, because a rigorous assessment is where seismic and geotechnical risk is supposed to be examined rather than assumed away.

Then there is the economics of the ore itself, which explains the scale of the disturbance. Gold grades at the deposit are low — a figure of roughly 0.78 grams per tonne has been cited by Armenian environmental specialists and is consistent with the company’s own data — which means that recovering a modest quantity of metal requires moving an enormous quantity of rock. The waste volume, whether one accepts the lower or higher published estimates, follows from that ratio. A low-grade deposit is not a reason to refuse a mine, but it does mean that the environmental footprint per unit of gold is large, and that the volume of material requiring permanent management is correspondingly immense.

\### \<strong>8. Four Routes Into the Water\</strong>

The pollution pathways at Amulsar are not vague anxieties; they can be described with some precision, which is why they are difficult to argue away. The first runs through the leach facility. Cyanide-bearing solution can leave containment through a failure in the pad liner, through overtopping when rainfall exceeds pond capacity, or through a rupture in the pipework that carries solution around the site. Because the pad sits partly within the drainage of a stream feeding the Arpa, any such release has a short and direct route into flowing water rather than a long overland path that would allow time for interception.

The second pathway is acid rock drainage, and it is the one that most worries specialists about legacy. Waste rock and exposed pit walls contain sulfide minerals; once mined and exposed to oxygen and water, they generate acid and release metals — arsenic, cadmium, lead among them — into whatever water passes through. The ELARD review found the project’s acid-generating calculations to be in error, which raises the question of whether the volume and reactivity of acid-forming material has been fully characterised. Unlike a spill, this process does not stop when operations cease.

The third pathway is groundwater. Open-pit mining requires dewatering, which changes the direction and rate of subsurface flow around the pit. Contaminants that enter the ground beneath the leach pad, the waste dumps or the water management ponds do not necessarily stay put; they travel with the water table. Armenian environmental analysts have pointed to government material acknowledging that seepage from the pits would move toward the Vorotan and Darb, which is an admission that the hydraulic connection between the site and the wider river system is real rather than hypothetical.

The fourth pathway is the one nobody plans for: catastrophic failure. The combination of seismic exposure, landslide susceptibility and substantial inventories of liquid cyanide on site defines a credible worst case in which a containment structure is breached suddenly and a concentrated pulse of contaminated water reaches the Arpa. The global record — the Mount Polley breach in British Columbia, the Baia Mare spill in Romania — shows that such events occur at operations that were considered compliant at the time. At Amulsar, the downstream geography includes populated settlements and ultimately the Lake Sevan system.

Against all this, the operator and the government maintain that monitoring and engineering controls are sufficient, that modelled seepage is small, and that the impact on river baseflow is negligible. The problem is not that these claims are implausible in principle. The problem is that the process by which the assessment underpinning them was adopted has been found unlawful by a court, that the forensic review identified calculation errors, and that the Bern Convention’s Bureau has repeatedly asked for the exercise to be redone. When the assurances rest on documents that have failed judicial and expert scrutiny, the assurances carry less weight than the risks they are meant to answer.

\### \<strong>9. The Water That Belongs to Everyone\</strong>

Lake Sevan is the reference point for everything in this dispute, and it is not merely a large body of water. It is Armenia’s principal freshwater reserve and a strategic resource for irrigation, hydropower and drinking supply, and it carries a weight in national life that is cultural as much as economic. Successive governments have treated the lake’s condition as a matter of state security, and the language of official documents — describing the lake’s ecological, economic, scientific, historical and spiritual significance — reflects how far beyond utility the asset is understood to extend.

What connects Amulsar to Sevan is engineering built in the Soviet period. The Arpa-Sevan and Vorotan-Arpa tunnels were constructed to arrest and reverse a catastrophic fall in the lake’s level, transferring water from the Arpa, Yeghegis and upper Vorotan catchments into the Sevan basin — on the order of 250 million cubic metres a year from the first system and around 165 million from the second. The Kechut and Spandaryan reservoirs, which sit in the Amulsar watershed, are treated in Armenian water law as part of the Sevan basin. The mine is therefore not adjacent to the national reservoir; it is inside the plumbing that feeds it.

The dependent sectors are not abstractions either. Agriculture in the Ararat Valley relies on irrigation releases from the Sevan system. Viticulture in Vayots Dzor and farming in Syunik draw on the Arpa and Vorotan. The Hrazdan cascade generates hydropower from the lake’s outflow. A contamination event in the upper Arpa would not remain an upstream curiosity; it would propagate through a connected system in which irrigation intakes, reservoir storage and drinking water supplies are all downstream of the same water.

Armenia also has direct experience of what happens when this system is mismanaged. The Soviet-era decision to draw down Lake Sevan for power and irrigation lowered the lake by close to twenty metres between 1949 and 1997, producing eutrophication, habitat destruction and biodiversity loss, and requiring decades of corrective engineering to begin reversing. The recovery is incomplete and the lake remains sensitive to nutrient and pollutant loads. Adding a persistent industrial source of cyanide, acid drainage and mobilised metals to a system still recovering from the last intervention is not a neutral act.

For the residents of Jermuk and Gndevaz, none of this is theoretical. The water is the reason the town exists, the reason visitors come, and the reason the local economy has a future that does not depend on a foreign mining company’s operating plan. For the rest of the country, the water is an inheritance held in trust, and the state’s obligation to protect it does not lapse because a project promises revenue. Amulsar, in the end, is a test of which of those two propositions the Armenian government actually believes. COP17 has concluded. The mine is open. The question has not been answered.

\<strong>Note on the Nature of This Article\</strong>

This article distinguishes between three categories of claim. \*\*Reported facts\*\* are those drawn from named sources and cited in the footnotes. \*\*Attributed claims\*\* are those presented as the position of a named party — the government, the company, civil society, or critics — and are identified as such in the text. \*\*Estimates\*\* are figures that vary across published accounts, such as waste-rock volume or the duration of potential pollution, and are flagged as contested rather than settled. The analytical judgments in the piece — about the significance of the legal sequence, the geography of benefit and burden, and the unresolved question of what the land is for — are the author’s own and are presented as argument rather than as findings of fact. The article does not meet the standards of a formal legal or scientific audit, and it is not intended to disparage any company, official, or person. For editorial standards, please consult the politics policy.

\<strong>Note on Local Testimony\</strong>

Residents of Jermuk, Gndevaz and neighbouring communities were approached for this article. Outreach was conducted through a Google Forms questionnaire, through direct email, and through local guide contacts via WhatsApp. Every individual who responded chose to remain anonymous. Not one person was willing to be named, or to have an opinion attributed to them in this article. That outcome is reported here as a factual finding about the conditions under which people closest to the mine are currently willing to speak publicly. The absence of named local voices is not an oversight by the author; it is a result of the reporting itself.
